# Krater — How the Gameplay, Mechanics, and Story Fit Together

*A companion to [The Hollow Primer](hollow-primer.md), which covers the story on its own. This doc is about the other half: why the mechanics are built the way they are, and how they were designed to make the story's themes actually playable rather than just told. Nothing here is a separate source of truth — where it conflicts with `mechanics-canon.md` or `story.md`, those win.*

---

## The one loop

Krater runs on a single core loop, reused everywhere rather than rebuilt per area:

**Live in the Hollow → take civic work or explore → travel, dig, extract, and haul → decide how far to push → keep, cache, deliver, order, or secretly divert → return to a Hollow that responds → improve your Rig, standing, and knowledge → reach new places.**

The same digging, hauling, and progression tools point in two directions at once, and that split is the whole game in miniature. Public work pushes **sideways**, into supervised civic galleries that expand the Hollow and produce Materials everyone can see. Secretly, the same tools can push **upward**, toward the one direction doctrine says is forbidden — the Firmament. A third, slower thread runs downward into the Devil's Mouth, a place that's obviously dangerous and needs no myth to keep people out. One mechanical system (digging), three meanings depending on direction. That's not a budget shortcut dressed up as theme — it's the actual point: the player is one person whose curiosity happens to point the one way nobody else's does, and the game expresses that with geography, not a cutscene.

## Pressure without combat

There's no combat in Act 1. Danger comes from stamina, fatigue, hauling capacity, civic time, commitments, and social consequence — never a monster. Overexert yourself digging too far from home and you don't die, you get stranded, exhausted, or forced into a costly rescue that other people notice and react to. Failure **continues the world in a changed state** rather than resetting it. Mechanically this keeps the game about judgment — how far to push, what to carry back, what to leave behind — instead of reflexes. Thematically it means every real risk in the game is a *social* risk: the thing you're actually afraid of isn't a creature, it's being found out, or letting someone down.

## The Hollow as a living place, not a menu

Three districts — Glowbeds, Wickwork, Cistern — each track a production rate (**Capacity**), what the Hollow currently needs from them (**Demand**), and a banked buffer of surplus (**Reserves**). Once per civic cycle, output gets produced, serves demand, and whatever's left tops up the buffer. A district can run healthy for a long stretch without babysitting; when something's short, it creates a specific, understandable problem — a delayed lift repair, thinner support out at the dig fronts — never a generic "-10 happiness" meter. There's no per-second production and no reward for standing around waiting for numbers to tick up.

This matters for the story more than it sounds like it should: the Hollow has to feel like a real, lived-in society whose people have real needs, or the whole premise — *everyone genuinely believes this is simply how the world is* — falls apart. A place that runs on visible meters reads as a game system. A place with production, shortages, and consequences that show up as actual changed conditions reads as somewhere people could plausibly have lived for generations without questioning it.

## Two paths for the same curiosity — Approved and Forbidden progression

Ordinary work earns **Tallies**, which the player spends to legitimately **order Approved Gear** through the Hollow's own channels — sanctioned equipment, openly built, openly worn. In parallel, the player can secretly **divert** part of a district's output — the same output legitimate orders draw from — into a hidden personal stash, and use it, along with recovered Materials and knowledge, to privately build **Forbidden Gear**. It's one shared economy with two very different front doors, not a separate "dark currency" bolted on.

This mechanical split *is* the Hollow's actual religion, not a theme sitting beside it. Working old salvaged technology by rote, ritual practice is called **Divine Binding**, and its sanctioned practitioners, the **Pulse Binders**, are treated as something close to clergy. The exact same underlying act, done without sanction — joining old technology to something — becomes **Hellbinding** (or Voidbinding) when it's done in secret. The forbidden version the player can eventually build isn't a separate power fantasy grafted on top of the setting; it's literally fusing recovered technology into the player's own body, using gear built for the colony ship's original pioneer corps — the **Firstwalkers** — never meant for anyone else. Folklore says going too far "traps your soul in the void." Given what it actually is, that might not be pure superstition.

## Discovery instead of a tech tree

There's no visible list of greyed-out upgrades waiting to unlock. Technology moves through three states — **Known** (you have a reason to know it exists), **Understood** (you have enough knowledge to make sense of it), **Available** (you also have what it actually takes to build it) — and it only ever advances because something in the world caused it to: a strange Component, a Record fragment, a Pulse Binder's offhand comment, an old system you happened to observe. This means the capability web and the lore are the same content, not two separate systems that happen to be themed alike. A Record you recover doesn't just unlock a mechanical option — reading it *is* how you learn a piece of the truth, and both things happen in the same moment for the same reason.

Build progression is steerable, not random — the world can point you toward a direction (a district project, a location, a recovered clue) if you want to lean into digging, hauling, mobility, or secrecy specifically, rather than everyone converging on one obviously-best build order.

## Trust, suspicion, and the cost of being seen

**Trust** is society's broad, qualitative opinion of the player's reliability — not a spendable currency, not a hidden morality score, and not something that moves for ordinary reasons. Routine work just earns Tallies. Trust only moves for things that actually mean something: broken commitments, being caught, exposed lies, real generosity, genuine above-and-beyond effort — always with a plain-language reason attached, never a silent number changing in the background.

**Suspicion** is a completely separate, local thing — it belongs to a specific person, place, or district, not to the player globally, and it comes from what actually happened: someone witnessing you somewhere you shouldn't be, an unexplained gap in a district's stock, a dug-out wall someone eventually finds. Undetected wrongdoing doesn't punish you just because the game itself knows about it — the world only reacts to what it actually has a reason to know, the same way a real society would.

There's no generic "Lie" button. Being questioned about something believable — late to a job, seen near a restricted dig, missing Ritual — opens real options: tell the truth, lie, deflect, or something in between, and what happens depends on what was actually witnessed or found, not a hidden dice roll. Trust earns you the benefit of the doubt in ambiguous moments, but it can never erase hard evidence once it's found.

One deliberate exception: **ordinary people cannot recognize Forbidden Gear for what it is.** A Warden, a neighbor, even a close friend — none of them have the specialized knowledge to look at a graft and know what they're seeing. Only the Council, or the First Steward himself, plausibly could. Practically, this means the player doesn't have to hide from every single person while carrying forbidden technology — the real danger is specifically being examined by someone who'd actually recognize it, which is rare by design. Thematically, it means the one person in the Hollow capable of ever truly seeing through the player's secret is the same person whose own secret the whole story is building toward. That's not a coincidence the mechanics happened to create — it's the reason this rule exists.

## Legitimate advancement is what opens the door to secrecy

The player climbs a real, visible housing ladder — **Lower → the Mid Reach → Ashram Heights** — entirely through legitimate means, Trust and Tallies earned by being a reliable, contributing member of the Hollow. And at every step, that legitimate climb is also what makes deeper secret progression *possible*: a proper workspace to actually divert goods into, then — once the Mid Reach is reached — the ability to graft at all, then, at Ashram Heights, literal physical proximity to the Firmament itself. The better a citizen you become, the more dangerous a heretic you're capable of being. This is stated outright as an intentional contradiction, not a side effect, and it's the same shape as Albus's own arc: the Hollow's most trusted elder is also its deepest heretic, having climbed exactly this same ladder himself, generations ago.

## The civic clock

**The Pulse**, a degraded ship relic at Mid Heart, sets the Hollow's rhythm — Rousing, Working, Gathering, and **Ritual**, the communal return anchor that closes each cycle. It's not a countdown the player races against; it's social texture. Missing Ritual isn't an automatic penalty, it's contextual — people notice, and how they react depends on whether you were expected, why you were gone, and whether this keeps happening. The Pulse Binders who maintain this rhythm are the same people practicing Divine Binding at large, which is why the Pulse sits at the literal and social center of the Hollow — the clock and the religion are the same institution.

## Jobs as an invisible tutorial

Work isn't one uniform quest type. Some of it is a real promise (**Accepted Work** or **Assigned Duty**, where failing actually costs something), and some of it genuinely isn't (**Available Work** you're free to ignore, **Emergency** situations nobody chose, **Personal Requests** with their own small social stakes) — and the game is careful never to punish the player for not helping with something they never actually promised to do. Early jobs walk the player through the game's real systems — dispatch, digging, extraction, hauling, delivery, Tallies, Ritual — through believable participation in a working society, rather than a tutorial popup layer sitting on top of it.

## How the acts build on all of this

**Act 1** happens entirely within the systems above: the Hollow's economy, the Approved/Forbidden split, Trust and Suspicion, the housing ladder, Divine Binding, all fully in play, culminating in the Firmament finally breaking open — a genuine surprise, never foreshadowed as a coming reveal.

**Act 2** reuses the exact same loop, reskinned onto the surface: venture out, gather, haul back, except now what you're hauling back also builds a surface settlement that recruits people (from the Hollow, and eventually from a second surviving population) using the same Trust-gated logic as everything else. The Devil's Mouth keeps deepening in parallel as a slower thread.

**Act 3** scales the identical capability-web-and-production architecture up to the largest possible project — building a ship home — and resolves on **two independent axes** rather than one: Trust and recruitment across every source decide *who leaves and how many*; build progression — how far into secret Hellbinding you went, which capabilities you actually developed — decides *what's reachable, and what happens to you specifically*. Both axes are just the same systems the player's been living in for the entire game, finally paying out.

## Why it's built this way

Nothing here is a meter for its own sake. District production, Trust, Suspicion, the capability web, the housing ladder — every one of them exists because the story needed a way to make an idea *playable*: a society you can believe has run this way for generations, a taboo with real teeth, a secret that costs something specific to keep, an advancement path that's genuinely good for you and genuinely dangerous at the same time. The test for every system in this game has always been whether it connects to the rest of the world and creates a real decision — never whether it looks good on a stat screen.
